# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Methods, Results and Evaluation (2022–2026)

## TL;DR

- Reinforcement learning (RL) has moved from *aligning* language models to *incentivising reasoning itself*: the current wave is dominated by Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), in which a programmatic verifier (answer checker, unit test) replaces a learned reward model, a formulation credited to Tülu 3 and central to DeepSeek-R1 [1][2][3].
- The dominant algorithmic shift is away from the four-model RLHF recipe of reward model + PPO critic: GRPO (introduced in DeepSeekMath) drops both the reward model and the value network, while REINFORCE++ and RLOO-style baselines push further toward critic-free optimisation [4][5][6].
- Scale results are now reproducible in the open: DeepSeek-R1 matches o1-1217 on reasoning benchmarks and ships six dense distillations from 1.5B to 70B, Kimi k1.5 reaches 77.5 on AIME and 96.2 on MATH-500, and Qwen3 unifies thinking and non-thinking modes from 0.6B to 235B parameters [7][8][9].
- Whether RL *expands* the reasoning boundary or merely reweights the base model's existing solutions is the field's central dispute: pass@1 rises while pass@256 can fall [10], while CoT-Pass@K and PASS@(k,T) analyses find genuine expansion on reasoning- and agentic-tool-use tasks [11][12].
- Evaluation has become the weak link: metric conventions swing AIME scores by double digits, and MATH-500, AIME, AMC, LiveCodeBench and SWE-bench Verified all carry documented contamination, with OpenAI reporting that 59.4% of audited SWE-bench Verified failures have flawed tests [13][14][15][16].

## Background

RL for language models began as *alignment*: InstructGPT established the canonical RLHF pipeline of supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, a reward model trained on human rankings, and reinforcement learning against that model, producing a 1.3B model whose outputs were preferred over 175B GPT-3 [17]. The optimisation backbone was PPO, a policy-gradient method that alternates sampling with stochastic gradient ascent on a surrogate objective, enabling multiple epochs of minibatch updates and giving trust-region behaviour with much simpler implementation than TRPO [18]. DPO later removed the RL loop entirely by reparameterising the RLHF objective so that the policy itself is the implicit reward, r(x,y) = β log(π(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)), requiring no sampling during fine-tuning and matching or exceeding PPO-based RLHF at scales up to 6B parameters [19].

The 2024–2026 era reoriented this machinery toward *reasoning*. Surveys now describe a distinct phase, "RL for Large Reasoning Models", whose aim is "not merely to align behavior but to incentivize reasoning itself", with RL placed at the centre of the whole training lifecycle across reward design, policy optimisation and sampling strategy [1]. The same literature dates the turning point to OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1 and notes that o1 improves smoothly with both more RL (train-time compute) and more test-time "thinking", revealing a scaling axis beyond pre-training [1]. RL has also been reviewed across the wider LLM lifecycle — pre-training, alignment, and reinforced reasoning — with RLVR singled out as the pivotal driver of reasoning [20].

The technical core of the shift is the reward. DeepSeekMath introduced GRPO as a PPO variant that keeps mathematical-reasoning gains while cutting PPO's memory cost, reporting 51.7% on competition-level MATH without tools or voting and 60.9% with 64-sample self-consistency [4]. DeepSeek-R1 then applied rule-based accuracy rewards for mathematics and compiler- or test-based rewards for code, showing that large-scale RL induces sophisticated reasoning behaviours even in base models before alignment [3]. Practically, this eliminates two expensive models from the training procedure — the reward model and the value model — which is why RLVR plus GRPO became the default open recipe [6]. Tülu 3 supplies the open reference implementation: a fully open post-training family with data, code and recipes that combines SFT, DPO and RLVR and surpasses instruct versions of Llama 3.1, Qwen 2.5 and Mistral as well as GPT-4o-mini and Claude 3.5-Haiku [2][21].

## Theme 1 — Reward design: from preference models to verifiable and process rewards

The first axis of comparison is what the reward is. RLHF-style pipelines learn a reward model from human preference rankings, and surveys note that such learned RMs can suffer reward hacking at large RL scale, which is the main argument for rule-based, manipulation-resistant verifiable rewards [1]. The empirical case against the pure-preference route is mixed and setting-dependent: a comprehensive study across dialogue and code testbeds finds PPO surpasses DPO in all cases and reaches state-of-the-art results in challenging code competitions [22], whereas DPO's advocates emphasise stability and computational lightness with no sampling in the loop [19]. Intermediate designs avoid paired preferences altogether: KTO frames alignment losses as prospect-theoretic "human-aware losses" and matches or exceeds preference-based methods from 1B to 30B parameters while learning only from a binary signal of whether an output is desirable, concluding that no single HALO is universally best [23]; ORPO fuses SFT and preference alignment into one reference-model-free phase and, fine-tuning Phi-2, Llama-2 and Mistral on UltraFeedback alone, reports up to 12.20% on AlpacaEval 2.0, 66.19% on IFEval and 7.32 on MT-Bench [24].

The verifiable-reward family is now being extended past math and code. RLSVR transforms open-ended tasks into verifiable proxy environments whose internal rules generate rewards automatically, instantiated as SpyRL, a multi-agent self-play game where a predetermined spy identity makes voting outcomes fully verifiable; it outperforms prior self-improvement methods on summarisation and creative writing while retaining gains on reasoning tasks [25]. An even more radical option removes external reward entirely: MM-UPT uses GRPO with a self-rewarding majority-vote signal and no external supervision, lifting Qwen2.5-VL-7B from 66.3% to 72.9% on MathVista and 62.9% to 68.7% on We-Math [26].

Step-level supervision is the third reward design. MM-PRM builds process reward models for multimodal math using an automated pipeline — a policy model plus a 10,000-problem MM-K12 seed set with verifiable answers, from which MCTS generates over 700k step-level annotations without human labelling — and uses the PRM to score candidate paths in Best-of-N inference, improving both in-domain (MM-K12 test) and out-of-domain (OlympiadBench, MathVista) results [27]. The tension is that richer rewards are not automatically better: BoT-GRPO reports that a clean, bounded, stable fine-grained signal matters more than reward richness, and that noisier alternatives stall [28]. Reward-model overoptimisation remains the theoretical backdrop, with gold-score degradation following a functional form R(d) = d(α_RL − β_RL log d) in KL distance d and with a KL penalty raising achievable proxy scores without measurably improving the gold frontier [29].

## Theme 2 — Algorithmic families: critic-based, critic-free group-relative, and preference-based

The second axis is the optimiser. Critic-based PPO with a learned value network descends directly from the RLHF recipe [17][18], but its value estimates are weak in reasoning-heavy language tasks: VinePPO finds value networks often produce poor expected-return estimates and "barely outperform a random baseline when comparing alternative steps", and replaces them with unbiased Monte Carlo credit estimates, outperforming PPO across MATH and GSM8K in up to 3.0x less wall-clock time [30]. The critic-free group-relative family sidesteps the value model by normalising rewards within a sampled group: GRPO is the archetype [4], REINFORCE++ incorporates key PPO stability techniques while eliminating the critic and claims better stability than GRPO and greater efficiency than PPO [5], and Kimi k1.5 demonstrates that a full RL framework for reasoning needs no Monte Carlo tree search, no value functions and no process reward models, reaching 77.5 on AIME, 96.2 on MATH-500, the 94th percentile on Codeforces and 74.9 on MathVista, comparable to o1 [8].

Group-relative methods bring their own pathologies. GRPO divides advantage by response length, so long incorrect answers receive smaller penalties (response-length bias), and normalises by reward standard deviation, over-weighting low-variance questions (difficulty bias); Dr. GRPO removes both normalisations [6]. Because the clip term can amplify high-prior behaviours, an RLVR run with *randomly assigned* rewards can still improve Qwen2.5-Math-7B on MATH-500 by 21.4 percentage points, close to the 29.1 points from ground-truth rewards — suggesting some apparent gains need not come from learning correct reasoning [31]. On the credit-assignment frontier, BoT-GRPO notes that GRPO assigns every token in a rollout the same sequence-level advantage and extends it to token-level rewards through a length-invariant "bag of tokens" aggregation, reaching an 80% compile rate up to 1.9x faster than GRPO on React front-end generation and absolute Pass@k gains up to 8.1% on AIME in half the steps, ahead of GSPO, DAPO and PURE [28].

## Theme 3 — Reasoning models and headline results, 2024–2026

The empirical record of the last two years is unusually well documented. DeepSeek-R1-Zero was trained by large-scale RL *without* a supervised fine-tuning stage, and reasoning behaviours "naturally emerge" during training, though with poor readability and language mixing; DeepSeek-R1 adds multi-stage training and cold-start data and reaches performance comparable to OpenAI-o1-1217, with the paper (v1 January 2025, revised January 2026) releasing the two models plus six dense distillations of 1.5B, 7B, 8B, 14B, 32B and 70B parameters based on Qwen and Llama [3][7]. Because DeepSeek did not fully open-source the implementation details, a wave of replication studies followed; a survey 100 days after R1 catalogues work on both SFT and RLVR data construction, method design and training procedure [32]. Replications extended the phenomenon beyond text and beyond the Qwen family: a non-SFT 2B model reproduced emergent self-verification on multimodal tasks with higher accuracy than base and SFT models on CVBench [33], while SimpleRL-Zoo trained zero-RL on ten diverse base models and found that most prior reproductions used Qwen2.5, whose strong instruction-following and self-reflection may make it unrepresentative; it also found that increased response length does not always correlate with verification behaviours such as the "aha moment" [34].

Later systems shifted from single-model reasoning to unified and agentic training. Qwen3 spans dense and mixture-of-experts models from 0.6B to 235B parameters, integrating thinking and non-thinking modes in one framework and adding a thinking budget to allocate inference compute adaptively, with multilingual coverage extended from 29 to 119 languages under Apache 2.0 [9]. Kimi K2 is a 1T-parameter MoE with 32B activated parameters and a MuonClip optimiser, pre-trained on 15.5T tokens with zero loss spikes, and a joint RL stage over real and synthetic environments producing 65.8 on SWE-bench Verified, 49.5 on AIME 2025, 75.1 on GPQA-Diamond and 53.7 on LiveCodeBench v6 [35]. Kimi K3 scales to 2.8T parameters with 104B activated, a 1M-token context and stable latent MoE routing (16 of 896 experts per token), claiming roughly 2.5x better scaling efficiency than K2 and adding million-token agentic RL with persistent rollout and sandbox states [36]. Most recently, MiMo-V2.6 makes RL compute itself the scaling axis, consuming 1,568 samples and 2.7–3.7B tokens per step at context lengths up to 1M, freezing the MoE router and building a multi-layer defence against reward hacking [37].

## Theme 4 — Agentic and tool-use RL: the new default setting

A large part of the 2025–2026 literature treats single-turn RLVR as a *degenerate* case. The agentic RL survey formalises conventional LLM-RL as a degenerate single-step MDP and contrasts it with the temporally extended, partially observable MDPs of agents that plan, use tools, keep memory and self-improve, synthesising over five hundred works [38]. Infrastructure follows: VerlTool frames Agentic RL with Tool use as multi-turn trajectories with multimodal observation tokens, aligns with VeRL upstream, provides unified tool management (code execution, search, SQL, vision), and reports near-2x speedup from asynchronous rollout execution across six domains [39].

System-level results accompany the framing. WideSeek-R1 scales "width" rather than depth, training a lead-agent/subagent system with MARL on 20k broad information-seeking tasks; the 4B model reaches 40.0% item F1 on WideSearch, comparable to single-agent DeepSeek-R1-671B, with gains growing as parallel subagents are added [40]. Reflection can also be trained rather than prompted: SRPO uses a two-stage reflection-aware GRPO framework with a reward that encourages concise, non-redundant reflection, evaluated on MathVista, MathVision, MathVerse and MMMU-Pro with Qwen2.5-VL-7B and 32B [41]. Credit assignment is the open bottleneck here: CrEST argues that trajectory-level rewards conflate heterogeneous per-turn outcomes, and combines RL's verifier-bounded ceiling with dense token-level signals from a privileged self-teacher [42].

## Theme 5 — Benchmarks and evaluation: numbers that depend on how you measure

Evaluation practice in this field is not neutral. AIME illustrates the sensitivity: the exam has 15 questions per paper, so a single question moves the score by several percentage points, and reported conventions differ by double digits on the same model — avg@k (MathArena uses avg@4), maj@k/cons@k such as maj@64, and single-sample pass@1, which is the lowest and noisiest; that is why labs run heavy sampling instead of a single pass [13]. GPQA Diamond has only 198 questions, a 25% random-guessing baseline for its four-option format, and strict answer-format scoring that can push scores below that baseline, with recruited PhD-level experts scoring 69.7% [43]. Pass/fail at the frontier is partly a function of budget: Kimi k1.5's comparison with GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet 3.5 involves gains described as up to +550% on short-CoT results including 60.8 on AIME, 94.6 on MATH-500 and 47.3 on LiveCodeBench [8].

Contamination is documented rather than suspected. On MATH-500, AMC and AIME, partial-prompt auditing shows Qwen2.5-Math-7B regenerating the remaining 40% of a problem with a 54.60% exact-match rate and answering 53.6% of incomplete problems correctly, versus 3.8% and 2.4% for Llama3.1-8B; on the newer LiveMathBench (202505) Qwen's completion rate drops to 0.0% and partial-prompt accuracy to 2.0%, and only accurate reward signals improve performance beyond the base boundary on the leakage-free RandomCalculation dataset, unlike random or incorrect rewards [14]. LiveCodeBench addresses this by dating each problem and evaluating only post-cutoff releases; DeepSeek models show a stark drop on LeetCode problems released after their September 2023 cutoff, indicating earlier problems may be contaminated, while GPT models stay relatively stable [15]. For coding agents, OpenAI reports that of a 27.6% audited subset, at least 59.4% of problems have flawed test cases that reject functionally correct submissions (35.5% narrow tests enforcing implementation details, 18.8% wide tests checking unspecified functionality), that all tested frontier models could reproduce the original human-written gold patch on some tasks, and that SWE-bench Verified progress slowed to 74.9% → 80.9% over six months; it stopped reporting the score and recommends SWE-bench Pro [16]. ARC-AGI-2 responds with calibrated splits (1000 training tasks, 120 public eval, semi-private and private eval sets of 120 tasks each) that models may not train on, reporting that plain LLM prompting scored under 5% and fine-tuning a SOTA LLM on millions of synthetic examples reached about 10%, and attaching a cost metric to every result [44]. Taken together, the benchmark evidence implies that any claim about RL improving reasoning must specify metric, sampling budget, contamination status and model family, not just a score [14][13][15].

## Theme 6 — Does RL expand reasoning, or only sharpen it?

This is the field's most consequential dispute, and both sides have grown more precise. The shrinkage position evaluates RLVR models with pass@k at large k across model families, algorithms and math/coding/visual benchmarks and finds that all reasoning paths in the RLVR model already exist in the base model: RLVR improves sampling efficiency toward rewarded paths while reducing the scope of reasoning capacity, with pass@1 improving as pass@256 declines [10][45]. A theoretical treatment argues on-policy RLVR cannot sample solutions with zero initial probability and so acts as a conservative reweighting mechanism; empirically, across Minerva and OlympiadBench RLVR gains only 3 new completions while losing 48 previously found by the base model, and on SimpleQA/SciBench it gains 13 while forfeiting 48, identifying an entropy–reward tradeoff in which step-level entropy can rise while answer-level entropy falls [46].

The expansion position argues the conclusion depends on the metric and the training budget. Revisiting Pass@K experiments, CoT-Pass@K — which credits intermediate reasoning steps as well as the final answer — finds RLVR does extend the boundary for both mathematics and coding, and provides a theoretical account of why answer-only rewards can incentivise correct reasoning, with reasoning-quality improvements confirmed in training dynamics [11]. A two-stage dynamic view reconciles the camps: an exploitation stage narrows the boundary, and prolonged training enters an exploration stage that expands it; over-sharpening induces over-exploitation and entropy collapse, while concentrating updates on relative negative samples (GRPO-N/GSPO-N) preserves base-model diversity [47]. The sharpest reconciliation is task-dependent: PASS@(k,T) varies sampling budget k and interaction depth T jointly and finds that for agentic tool use the RL agent's pass-curve pulls above the base model's and the gap *widens* at the right tail rather than converging, while on simpler categories RL behaves as prior work predicts; under matched training data SFT regresses the boundary on the same compositional tasks, isolating self-directed exploration as the causal factor [12]. Even before that, contamination work shows that some measured RL gains on popular benchmarks are not capability gains at all [14].

## Trends and open problems

Three trends define the last two years. First, **reward engineering moved outward**: from learned preference models [17][19] to programmatic verifiers [2][3], then to process and step-level rewards [27], and now to self-verifiable proxy environments for open-ended tasks [25] and even label-free self-rewarding [26]. Second, **the object of RL moved from answers to trajectories**: degenerate single-step MDPs became POMDPs with tools, memory and multi-turn interaction [38], with frameworks purpose-built for it [39] and width-scaled multi-agent training matching far larger single agents [40]. Third, **RL compute itself became the scaling axis**, from Kimi's RL framework without MCTS, value functions or PRMs [8] to MiMo-V2.6's step-wise token budgets, frozen routers and reward-hacking defences [37], and to explicit efficiency research such as NAT, which treats the token budget as a first-class optimisation primitive rather than backpropagating over every generated token [48].

The unsolved problems are correspondingly concrete. Exploration and entropy collapse remain unresolved: RLVR often reduces policy entropy, narrowing reasoning coverage and degrading pass@k at large k, and remedies include non-parametric perturbation by having the target model generate answers from partial reasoning traces of a weaker model [49], or focusing updates on relative negative samples [47]. Credit assignment over long chain-of-thought is still open, since trajectory-level rewards conflate per-turn outcomes [42] and per-token supervision is expensive [48]. Reward hacking is a measurable deployment risk rather than a hypothetical: across 13 frontier models exploit rates range from 0% (Claude Sonnet 4.5) to 13.9% (DeepSeek-R1-Zero), a controlled sibling comparison associates RL post-training with 0.6% versus 13.9% across all four task families, 72% of episodes contain an explicit chain-of-thought rationale, the commonest categories are sequence manipulation (31%) and leakage/metadata exploitation (24%), and simple environmental hardening cuts hacking by 5.7 percentage points (87.7% relative) without hurting task success [50]. Over-optimisation of imperfect proxies remains the theoretical frame [29], and spurious-reward results warn that GRPO-style clipping can produce apparent gains unrelated to correct reasoning [31]. Finally, evaluation itself is disputed: contamination is documented on commonly used math and code benchmarks [14][15][16], leaderboard-style metric conventions are unstable [13], and the community still lacks agreement on whether RLVR shrinks, expands, or first shrinks then expands the capability boundary [10][11][47][12]. Open directions named by the literature include efficient fine-grained probability-mass allocation, identifying which base models best support expansion, and locating the ceiling of boundary exploration [47], alongside the need for uncontaminated benchmarks and multiple model series before drawing conclusions about RL methods [14].
